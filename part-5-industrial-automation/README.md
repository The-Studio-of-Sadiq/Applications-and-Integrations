# Part V — Industrial Automation

Factories, water works, quarries and process plants run on a layer of engineering the theory books mention only in passing: the controller that scans its inputs every 10 ms, the I/O drop that goes to a designed safe state when its network falls silent, the 4–20 mA loop whose voltage budget decides whether a reading is true, the drive that must stop a belt within 4 s, and the networks and safety systems that tie them together. This part designs that layer. It follows four real systems from requirement to commissioning: the control system of a 30 ML/day water-treatment works, the instruments and actuators of a dairy clean-in-place skid, the motor control of a quarry conveyor line, and the networks and safety functions of a palletising cell on a beverage line. The theory is taken from the books that own it; this part adds the architecture, the budgets, the real parts, the PLC code, the tests with their limits, and the failures each design had to survive.

## By the end of this part you should understand

- How an industrial control system is layered and partitioned into PLCs, remote I/O, SCADA, HMIs and networks, and when a DCS is the better answer.
- How to budget a PLC's response time, a telemetry network's data age, a redundant controller's availability and a panel's 24 V and heat loads, each with a margin.
- How proximity sensors, limit switches, RTDs, pressure transmitters, flowmeters, solenoid valves, contactors, relays and actuators are chosen and interfaced, and why 4–20 mA loop budgets, sensing distances and coil suppression matter.
- How motors are started, protected, speed-controlled, interlocked and stopped, and how starter type, current limit, trip class and supply dip are decided together.
- How emergency stops are built as independent functions with stop categories, safety relays and safe torque off, and validated by measured stopping times.
- How field networks are sized and diagnosed, how safety functions reach a required performance level, and how a plant is secured and documented.
- How commissioning (FAT, SAT, loop checks, E-stop validation) turns a correctly built system into a trusted one.
